Annotation guidelines

This is the field manual — what to do, in what order, and how to decide the cases that aren't obvious. If you're an SME doing the marking, this document is written for you, not for the engineering team. Every rule below has a worked example. Where a call is genuinely a judgment call, that's said plainly, and it's what the calibration round (§08) exists to catch before it costs anything.

01 What you're doing, and why it matters

We're measuring how well an automated system finds people, organizations, and their contact details in claim notes. To measure that, we need a small set of notes where a human has already found everything — that set is what every other number in this project gets compared against. If it's sloppy, every number built on it is sloppy, including the number that tells the client whether this system is worth buying.

You are not grading the system. You will not see what it found. You are reading the note as if the system doesn't exist, and marking what's actually there. That's the whole job for most of what you'll do — the rest is verifying or correcting things a machine already located, which is much faster and explained separately below.

The one thing that matters more than speed

If you're not sure, mark it "probable" and move on — never skip it and never guess "certain." A skipped mention makes the system look worse than it is. A wrongly-certain mention makes it look better than it is. "Probable" is the honest answer to "I think so but I'm not sure," and it's tracked separately for exactly this reason. See §05.

02 The five task types

You'll be assigned one task type at a time through the review tool — you don't choose which note you see or what task it is. Here's what each one looks and feels like.

T1 · Cold annotation

the core task — most of your hours go here
see A blank note. No highlights, no hints, nothing pre-marked.
do Read it once for context. Then go through and mark every person, organization, and identifier (phone/email/address/ID number) exactly as described in §03–§04.
do When you reach the end, mark the document complete. This one click is what allows this note to be used for measuring precision later — don't skip it, and don't mark complete if you were interrupted partway through.
You record: a span (drag or click-to-click), an entity type, and a certainty (§05).

T2 · Located-span verify

fast — a few seconds each
see One span already highlighted in the note, in context.
do Decide: is the highlight in the right place, referring to the right entity?
You record one of: correct / wrong span (then drag the right one) / not this entity.

T3 · Not-found triage

an entity name, and the whole note to search
see A name from the client's records (e.g. "Marisol Vega") and the full text of one note it's supposedly on.
do Search the note. Is she actually named in the text somewhere our matching missed? Referred to without her name ("the claimant's daughter")? Or genuinely absent — maybe she's only in the system's metadata, not this note's prose?
You record: found here (mark the span) / not textually present / referenced without a name.

T4 · Null-metadata recovery

this is the week-one deliverable — see it as validating a specific, checkable claim
see A phone number, address, or ID our system found in the note text, and the entity it's plausibly attached to (whose record has that field blank).
do Read the sentence it's in. Does this identifier actually belong to this entity?
You record: belongs / does not belong / unsure.

T5 · Adjudication

only if you're annotating alongside a second SME
see A place where you and another annotator marked the same note differently.
do Look at both markings side by side and decide which is right — or if both are, or neither.
You record: pick A / pick B / neither / both.

03 Is this an entity? — the decision tree

We're marking every person and every organization named or clearly referenced in the note — every party, not just the claimant and their attorney. Work through this in order for anything you're unsure about.

Does this refer to a specific person or organization — not a category?
No → skip"the adjuster's office," "our legal team" as a generic group, "insurance company" used generically
Yes → continuea specific name, or a role clearly pointing at one identifiable individual
Is it named directly (a proper name, or "Dr. ___," a firm name)?
Yes → mark, certain"Robert Miller," "Alicia Reyes, MD," "Whitfield Trial Group"
No → continuea pronoun, a role-only phrase, a descriptor
Does a role-only phrase point at exactly one person in context ("the adjuster," "her attorney")?
Yes → mark, probableunless the note makes it unambiguous, then certain
No → skiptoo vague to attribute to one entity

Worked examples

Spoke with Robert Miller regarding the POA update. He confirmed the treating provider remains Alicia Reyes, MD at the Lakeshore clinic. Claimant's attorney continues to be D. Alvarez, Esq. of Whitfield Trial Group.
greennamed directly — mark certain
amberpronoun/role pointing at one clear person — mark probable
strucka place, not an entity — do not mark
Identifiers get marked too, separately from names

A phone number, email, mailing address, or ID number is its own mark, even when it's sitting right next to a name — don't fold it into the name span. And critically: mark an identifier even when no name is anywhere nearby. "Callback left at (312) 555-0148, no name given" — mark the phone number on its own. This is the single case the system exists to get right, so it's the single case most worth getting right here too.

04 Boundary rules

Where exactly a mark starts and ends. Each of these was an open question until calibration settled it — if you hit a case not covered here, mark your best judgment, flag it with a note, and it becomes a rule in v2 (§08).

includeTitles: Dr. Reyes, not Dr. Reyes split apart.
includeSuffixes: Robert Miller Jr. — the whole thing is one mark, not two people.
includeOrg suffixes: Whitfield Trial Group LLC — the legal-entity suffix stays in the span.
excludePossessive 's: mark Robert Miller's policy, not Robert Miller's policy — the 's is not part of the name.
mark, probableRole-only references with no name: the treating physician ordered an MRI — mark it, tier probable, per §03.
mark every occurrenceQuoted/forwarded email chains: if the same mention appears three times in a quoted thread, mark all three — flag them as quoted so they can be included or excluded from a metric later.
excludeSentence-initial verbs that happen to be capitalized: "Contacted James Moore" — James Moore is the mark, "Contacted" is not part of it.
The most common real mistake

Swallowing a sentence-opening word into the name because both are capitalized — "Contacted James Moore," "Spoke with Alicia Reyes." Read the sentence, not just the capitalization pattern, before setting your boundary.

05 Certainty: certain vs. probable

Every mark carries one of two tiers. This is not optional and it is not a hedge — it's the mechanism that keeps a genuinely ambiguous mention from being silently scored as if it were obvious.

certain You would stake your judgment on it without hesitation. A named person or org, a role reference with only one possible referent.
probable You believe it, but a reasonable second reader might not mark it, or might mark it differently — an ambiguous pronoun, a role phrase with two possible referents nearby, a partly-legible fragment.

Every metric that comes out of this dataset is reported twice — strict (certain only) and lenient (certain + probable). Both numbers matter; neither is "the real one." That's why the tier has to be honest rather than optimistic.

06 Hard-case tags

Attach any that apply to an entity while you're marking it. These aren't required — add them when you notice the pattern, don't go hunting for them.

jr_srJr/Sr suffix conflict — two entities at the same address differing only by suffix.
shared_addressTwo distinct entities share one address.
recycled_phoneA phone number that seems to have moved from one entity to another over time.
address_changeThis entity's address changed between notes.
identifier_reassignedAn identifier appears to have moved from one entity to another.
high_fanoutThis entity (usually an attorney or provider) recurs across an unusual number of claims.
phoenix_shopA repair shop that appears to have reopened under a new name at an old address.

07 Common mistakes

  • Marking complete on a note you skimmed. If you're not confident you read every line, don't mark it complete — leave it in progress. A falsely-complete note quietly corrupts every precision number built on it.
  • Splitting one mention into two marks (title, or suffix, separated from the name) — see §04.
  • Treating "certain" as the default and only downgrading when actively unsure. Ask the reverse question: would a second reader agree without discussion? If not, it's probable.
  • Silently resolving ambiguity yourself. If a pronoun could point at two different people and you're guessing, that's exactly what "probable" is for — don't quietly pick one and mark it certain.
  • Skipping identifiers with no attached name. This is the highest-value case in the whole dataset — see the callout in §03.

08 Calibration, and what happens to disagreements

Before any note counts toward the real gold set, two SMEs annotate the same 30 notes independently — neither sees the other's marks. We measure how often you agreed (Cohen's κ). If it's below 0.75, that's not a grade on either annotator: it means this document has an ambiguous rule in it, and we fix the document, not the people.

Every disagreement from that round goes through T5. The resolution — and the reason for it — becomes a new rule here, in v2. If you hit a case during real annotation that isn't covered above, mark your best judgment, add a note explaining the call, and expect to see it addressed in the next revision.

09 Quick reference

Cheat sheet

Mark it
  • Named person or org → certain
  • Role/pronoun with one clear referent → probable (certain only if truly unambiguous)
  • Any phone / email / address / ID number → its own mark, always, even with no name nearby
Don't mark it
  • A place, not a person or org
  • A generic category ("the insurance company," "our office")
  • Sentence-opening verbs swallowed into a capitalized name
Boundary
  • Include: titles, suffixes (Jr./Sr.), org suffixes (LLC, Group)
  • Exclude: possessive 's
  • Mark quoted/forwarded duplicates every time they appear
When unsure
  • Mark probable, never skip, never guess certain
  • Add a note — it becomes a rule for everyone next revision
